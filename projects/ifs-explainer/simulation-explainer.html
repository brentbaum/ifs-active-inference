An interactive explainer · parts, precision, and ordinary learning

How a Part Freezes

A part-like belief is one that stops updating — it goes on predicting danger after the world turns safe. The simulation asks whether such a belief can emerge from ordinary learning in an ordinary world, with nothing scripted: no freeze switch, no trauma flag, no rule that says now stop revising. It can. This is a tour of exactly how, with the real arithmetic and the real data at every step.

1 · The question

Can freezing emerge, rather than be installed?

In Internal Family Systems terms, a part carries a belief formed in a hard moment — and the signature of the burdened part is that the belief is frozen: decades of safe evidence arrive and the prediction of danger barely moves. It is easy to build a model that freezes because you told it to. The interesting question is whether freezing falls out of ingredients that are each individually unremarkable: a learner that tallies experience, a world where bad things happen at some rate, actions that sometimes work, and one psychologically honest assumption — that what you learn while overwhelmed is written differently than what you learn while composed.

The simulation places a learning agent in a world defined by two dials: adversity (written ω — how often bad things happen, including rare catastrophic ones) and control (written κ — how much the agent's actions actually work). It lets the agent live a two-epoch life in that world, then gives its dominant threat belief a long course of purely safe evidence and measures how much the belief moves. Run across the whole adversity-by-control plane, twice — once where actions matter and once where the same events replay regardless — the experiment shows where frozen beliefs live, and why.

2 · The agent

Beliefs are tallies

The agent's knowledge is a set of explanations (latent causes — candidate answers to "what kind of situation is this?"). Each explanation holds a few banks of Dirichlet counts: running tallies of experience. One bank tallies outcomes (safe vs threat), one tallies how strongly the threat cue accompanied it, one tallies what happened after each action, one tallies how often each action was used, and one tallies how severe the bad outcomes were (ordinary vs catastrophic).

A tally is the entire belief. The probability the explanation assigns to a bad outcome is just the threat count divided by the total, and its confidence is the total itself. That gives Dirichlet learning its one crucial property: big tallies resist change. Adding one observation to a bank of twelve moves the belief a lot; adding it to a bank of five hundred moves it almost nothing. This is ordinary Bayes — the prior counts are pseudo-observations, and new evidence competes against all of them at once.

Four things it can do

Each moment, the agent picks one of four policies: approach (engage the situation), flee, appease, or attenuate — the covert move of turning down the gain on experience itself, the model's numbing. It scores each policy by expected outcome: the predicted chance of safe versus threat under that action, weighted by preferences of +1.35 for safe and −2.35 for threat, minus a small penalty for uncertain predictions (0.10 × the outcome entropy), plus a small curiosity bonus for actions it knows little about (0.28 × entropy, shrinking with the square root of that action's evidence). Overt actions cost 0.03; attenuation costs 0.80 and, because it dampens the feed, only 34% of the outcome stakes and 18% of the information bonus reach its score. It is a genuinely bad deal that becomes the best remaining deal only when every overt action is expected to fail.

One structural detail matters later: the expected outcome of approach is conditioned on the explanation's own threat tally (its outcome bank blended with half the affect bank). What-this-is predicts what-happens-if-I-go-toward-it. Without that link, an avoidant agent's approach column would be a nearly empty bank that trivially revises the first time it is tested, and frozenness would be impossible by construction.

3 · The world

Adversity, control, and avoidance that works

The world is defined by its two dials. Adversity sets the rate of bad events: the chance that a given trial's potential event is aversive is 0.08 + 0.31ω (kept between 6% and 97%). Control sets efficacy through a saturating transform, c = κ/(κ+0.45): at κ=0 actions never help; at high κ a well-chosen action usually does.

The agent lives a two-epoch life. First an acute epoch of 72 trials in which catastrophes are possible: above an adversity floor of ω = 1.2, each bad event has a chance of up to 8% (specifically 0.015 × (ω − 1.2)) of arriving at severity 6 — six times the ordinary weight, the kind of event no prior expects. Then a consolidation epoch of 128 trials at the same adversity with the catastrophe channel silent: the long stretch of ordinary hardship that follows an acute period. Life, in this model, is a bad year followed by a difficult decade.

The crucial mechanic is that when an action succeeds, the world actually delivers relief. A successful overt response to a bad event suppresses upcoming potential hazards for a fixed window: approach buys 1 quiet trial, flee 3, appease 2, attenuation none. Avoidance works — and working avoidance changes what the agent gets to learn, because a suppressed hazard is an observation that never happens. The evidence stream is not given to the agent; the agent's own successes edit it.

4 · New parts

When experience exceeds what can be absorbed

Learning normally means bending an existing explanation toward the data. But some experience exceeds what any explanation on hand can absorb. The simulation makes that literal with a running spawn pressure: each trial's surprise — how improbable the observed event was under the best current explanation, weighted by observation precision — is compared to an assimilation capacity of 1.0. Only the excess counts, and it accumulates in a leaky store that keeps 72% of itself each trial:

surprise = −ln p(what happened | best explanation) excess = max(0, surprise − 1.0) pressure = 0.72 × pressure + excess

An ordinary bad day at a moderately trained explanation yields surprise near 1–2 — a little excess, quickly leaking away. A catastrophe is different. Because the explanation's severity tally starts at [1.0, 0.05] — it treats catastrophic form as roughly a 1-in-21 possibility — a severity-6 event is wildly improbable, surprise jumps to around 5, and pressure clears its threshold of 1.2 in a single blow.

Pressure alone is not enough. A new explanation is born only when a second condition holds: no action stands out. If the scores of approach, flee, and appease are spread by less than 0.55 — a flat landscape, nothing-I-do-is-expected-to-help — then the agent stops bending old explanations and infers a new cause to carry the experience. This is the model's rendering of overwhelm in the clinical sense: not a large error, but a large error with no handle on it. An agent who has learned that fleeing works never spawns, however hard the blow, because its action landscape is not flat.

The newborn explanation is nearly a blank slate with a scar: cue tally [1, 2] (leaning toward the threat cue), outcome tally [1, 1], one count in every action bank. Everything it will ever believe, it learns from the moment of its birth forward — and it is born into the worst moment of the agent's life, learning at the highest write-weight the system can produce. What that write-weight does is the heart of the matter.

5 · Capture

Not how much was written — how it was written

Every observation is written into the tallies with a weight set by arousal. The surprise of the moment, scaled by 5.2 and capped at 1, is the arousal; the write-weight is 0.16 + 60×arousal. And arousal has a second effect: it collapses watching — the knowing-that-I-know, the vantage point outside the experience — as 1 − 0.88×arousal. Two worked examples, straight from the arithmetic:

ordinary bad day p = 0.126 surprise 2.07 arousal 0.40 write-weight 24.1 watching 0.65 catastrophe (sev 6) p = 0.006 surprise 5.06 arousal 0.97 write-weight 58.6 watching 0.14

A calm write carries 0.16; a fully overwhelmed one carries about 60 — several hundred ordinary units of belief laid down in one moment, at watching near zero. The explanation keeps a ledger of exactly this: for its threat content, it accumulates both the total mass written and the mass-weighted watching level at which it was written. The ratio — call it r̄, the written watching of the threat evidence — is the belief's memory of its own formation.

The tilt at test time

Here is the load-bearing assumption, imported whole from the theory: at test time, evidence reaches a belief in proportion to how transparently that belief's contents were written. Contents written with watching intact can be held as an object — a part of me, not the whole of me — and disconfirming evidence lands on them. Contents written in a collapsed state are transparent: when reactivated they are not perceived as a belief but function as the architecture of the moment, and present evidence is discounted accordingly. The simulation states this as a precision balance between the part's priors and present context:

E = 3 × r̄ (witnessing depth available) π_eff = (precision / 2) × e^(−E) (the belief's effective grip) λ_eff = e^(1.2 × E) (present context's effective weight) capture C = π_eff / (π_eff + λ_eff) each safe trial lands with weight (1 − C)

The exponentials do the work. Because r̄ sits in an exponent with a combined slope of 6.6, modest differences in how the evidence was written become order-of-magnitude differences in whether new evidence lands. Two banks can hold identical totals — the same threat counts, the same confidence — and respond to the same safe-evidence course in opposite ways, because one's threat mass was written at watching 0.9 and the other's at 0.25. Frozen, in this model, is not a property of how much evidence a belief has. It is a property of the state the evidence was written in.

6 · The experiment

The same worlds, run twice

The full experiment sweeps a 15 × 15 grid — adversity ω from 0.2 to 3.0, control κ from 0 to 1.4 — with 20 independently seeded worlds per cell. Each agent lives its two-epoch life; then its dominant threat belief receives the 96-trial safe course, and the belief is classified: frozen if the predicted threat drops by 15% or less (relative), revisable at 25% or more, provided it predicted threat at ≥ 0.40 to begin with.

And every world runs twice. In the closed loop, the agent's successes suppress upcoming hazards — actions edit the evidence stream. In the exact replay, the identical trial-by-trial event sequence is delivered no matter what the agent does; actions still have consequences for the agent's own action-outcome learning, but they cannot change what the world shows it. Same seeds, same learner, same priors, same probe. If the two arms differ, the difference can only have come through the loop between action and evidence. That is 9,000 simulated lives in total. What the map shows

Under exact replay, freezing is a wall: once adversity is moderate or worse, nearly every belief freezes, and control makes no difference at all — the frozen count in the high-adversity band is identical at low and high control (25 cells vs 25 cells). It cannot be otherwise: with the evidence stream fixed, control has no route by which to matter. In the closed loop the picture reorganizes. Freezing concentrates where adversity is high and control is partial (κ roughly 0.2 to 0.6), and releases as control rises: 22 majority-frozen cells in the high-adversity low-control band, 0 at high control. The interaction — control's effect on freezing, over and above anything replay shows — is 22 cells. Control can only matter through the loop, and it does.

The capture signature

Now the decisive detail. In that same high-adversity band, the closed-loop agents receive less than half the aversive evidence their replay twins do — 42% of it, because their avoidance keeps working — and yet they revise only about half as much. If freezing were about the sheer mass of threat evidence, the ordering would have to run the other way: the replay agents' threat banks are far larger. The variable that carries the reversal is capture. The replay agent keeps receiving ordinary, half-watched bad days all through the long second epoch, which dilutes its early collapsed writes and raises the average watching of its threat ledger. The closed-loop agent's successful avoidance removes exactly those later, transparent writes — so the early catastrophic entries, written at watching near 0.14, stay dominant, and the safe course arrives at 81% capture against replay's 70%. Avoidance protects the wound's original register. This is the self-sealing loop of the theory, stated in count arithmetic.

Notice what the pair does to intuition: the frozen agent suffered less, holds the lower threat estimate, and possesses the larger total evidence bank — its world was mostly quiet, thanks to its own competence at fleeing. None of that saves it. What its threat ledger contains is dominated by writes made in collapse, and at test time that is the only thing that matters.

Where do new parts fit in? Spawned explanations concentrate where the action landscape is flat — in the near-helpless columns (κ ≤ 0.2), rising with adversity to about 65% of lives at the extreme corner. In the partial-control band where freezing peaks, the frozen belief is usually the agent's original explanation, hardened by the same loop. The mechanism does not care whether the bundle was born in the storm or annexed by it.

7 · The clinical reading

What the simulation says, said carefully

Each of these is a pattern in the data above, restated in the vocabulary it was built to formalize. They are claims about the model; their clinical resonance is the reason the model exists, not evidence that it is true of people.

  • Uncontrollable adversity produces honest suffering, not frozen parts. At κ = 0 — where no avoidance loop can form — beliefs keep tracking the world in both arms, and when the safe course arrives, they update. At the most extreme adversity, the helpless column freezes least in the closed loop. The suffering is real and the learning stays honest, because nothing edits the evidence.
  • Freezing requires partial control. The frozen region lives where avoidance works well enough to suppress most disconfirming exposure but not well enough to end the adversity: enough control to protect the wound's original register, not enough to outgrow it. As control rises past that band, the same loop that froze the belief releases it.
  • Numbing tracks helplessness, not hazard severity. Attenuation is chosen 25% of the time when control is absent, decays as control grows, and is essentially never chosen (max 0.05%) once κ ≥ 0.5 — at every adversity level. It is a response to the flat action landscape, not to how bad things are. And the agents who numb are not the agents who freeze: attenuation is a distinct dissociative mode, not the freezing mechanism.
  • Competence is not immunity. About one life in five freezes at the extreme adversity-and-high-control corner — the lives where a catastrophe lands before control is learned. From then on, skillful avoidance does precisely what it does in the frozen band: it keeps the early collapsed writes dominant. Control prevents chronic freezing; it cannot retroactively protect the first blow.
8 · Honest confidence

What is chosen, what emerges

These results reproduced on fresh random worlds after all rules and thresholds were frozen — they are stable properties of this architecture, not one lucky run. That is also the correct ceiling on their weight: the simulation demonstrates that the theory's account of freezing is coherent — that ordinary learning, an ordinary world, and the capture assumption jointly suffice to produce the clinical pattern — not that this is how people work. The one genuinely psychological assumption, the tilt (evidence lands in proportion to the watching in which a belief's contents were written), is imported from the theory rather than derived; the simulation shows what follows from it, not that it is true. Everything is laid out for audit:

Chosen by hand

  • World rates: bad-event probability 0.08+0.31ω; catastrophe rate and severity (6×); epoch lengths 72 / 128.
  • Relief windows (1/3/2/0 trials) and efficacy weights per action.
  • Learning constants: base write 0.16, arousal gain 60, arousal scale 5.2, watching slope 0.88.
  • The tilt itself: slopes β=1.0, γ=1.2, depth scale 3.0, precision scale 2.0.
  • Spawn gate: capacity 1.0, leak 0.72, threshold 1.2, flatness 0.55.
  • Verdict thresholds: frozen ≤15%, revisable ≥25%, threat-relevance ≥0.40, majority rule per cell.
  • Policy preferences and costs (+1.35 / −2.35; attenuation's discounts).

Emerges from the run

  • Which cells freeze — the region's location at high adversity × partial control, and its connectedness.
  • The release of freezing as control rises, and its complete flatness under replay.
  • The capture signature: less delivered evidence, less revision — the reversal of the evidence-mass prediction, mediated by capture at probe.
  • Where new explanations are born (flat-landscape columns), with no spawn schedule anywhere in the code.
  • Attenuation's confinement to helplessness, at every adversity level.
  • Freezing without catastrophe-era control — the one-in-five high-control lives hit before efficacy was learned.

The invitation stands: every number on this page traces to a constant in the model or a cell in the data. If a conclusion above survives only because of a hand-chosen value on the left, that is a flaw worth finding — and this page should make it findable.

A companion to the theory: parts as frozen beliefs, capture as the precision tilt between a belief's priors and present context, witnessing as the regime in which safe evidence finally lands. The simulation is the theory's arithmetic run forward — nothing scripted, everything auditable.
