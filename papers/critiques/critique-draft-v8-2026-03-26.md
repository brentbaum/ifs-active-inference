## Four-Axis Diagnosis

### A. Conceptual advance
**Verdict: Strong | Severity: Minor issues only**

What changes because this paper exists: Three things. (1) "Part" gets a formal definition — identity-level precision bundle — that is not merely a relabeling of schema, latent context, or trait prior. The discriminant validity paragraph in Section 3 earns this. (2) Self-energy becomes a tractable, operationalizable variable rather than a clinical intuition. (3) Relational prediction error is named as a specific mechanism with a specific computational address (the organizing prior of the bundle), distinguishing IFS change from exposure at the algorithmic level.

The novelty is concentrated and memorable. An intelligent adjacent scholar could state the contribution in one sentence: "Parts are precision bundles with self-state upstream, and relational prediction error under witnessing is what reaches the root." That is a good sign.

The advance is real. The discriminant validity paragraph (Section 3) is load-bearing and well-executed — it names three predictions that nearby constructs do not make. The relational prediction error mechanism (Section 8.3) is the paper's deepest new idea and is well-argued.

Minor concern: Move 3 (relational prediction error) is the most novel contribution, but it is introduced in Section 8.3 — late in the paper. Moves 1 and 2 take up the majority of the text. The paper's center of gravity is front-loaded toward the less novel contributions.

### B. Narrative force
**Verdict: Good with structural issues | Severity: Moderate**

The paper generates genuine pressure for its move. The opening three sentences are excellent. The dog example is concrete and returns throughout the paper, grounding each abstraction. The translation table (Table 1) is well-placed.

The argumentative spine is visible and mostly strong. Each section advances the argument. The paper introduces one idea at a time through Sections 1-6. Then it pays off the setup in Sections 7-8.

Three structural issues:

1. **Section 8.3 arrives too late and carries too much weight.** The relational prediction error mechanism is arguably the paper's most original contribution (it is what distinguishes IFS from exposure at the mechanistic level, not just at the regime level). But it arrives 60% of the way through the paper, after 8 sections of setup. The reader has been told since the abstract that relational prediction error matters, but does not encounter the argument until deep into the paper. The introduction promises it. Sections 3-7 defer it. By the time it arrives, the reader has already built a mental model of the paper's contribution (parts as bundles + Self-energy as governor) and Section 8.3 asks them to revise it. The section should feel like the climax. Instead it feels like an addition.

2. **Sections 10-11 (simulation design and results) are not yet updated to reflect the v2 simulation.** The current text describes results that match the v1 simulation. The paper mentions threshold crossings at specific trial numbers, cascade ordering, and specific figure references, but the simulation section has not been updated to reflect the v2 results (per the user's note). This is a known gap, but it means the simulation sections currently cannot be evaluated for narrative force. They need to be rewritten once.

3. **The conclusion (Section 12) is competent but does not gather.** It enumerates six things the model explains, lists what it does not explain, states implications, and lists next steps. It is organized as a checklist rather than as an arrival. The final sentence — "It builds the first floor. That is enough, at least for now, if the floor holds." — is modest and honest but does not achieve the "beautiful ending" the paper deserves. The paper has earned more than that. After 10,000 words of careful argument, the reader should feel the recognition click, not just the floor settle.

### C. Cross-field legibility
**Verdict: Strong | Severity: Minor issues only**

The paper is remarkably legible for a theory paper that bridges clinical psychology and computational neuroscience. The prose is clean. Technical terms are introduced exactly when needed. The precision definition in Section 3.1 ("how much the system trusts a given source of information") is a model of accessibility. The dog example does continuous work throughout.

The abstract reveals significance clearly. It names the three moves, the simulation results, and the empirical target in one paragraph. It is slightly long (one paragraph, dense) but does its job.

Jargon is controlled. The paper uses "precision," "prior," "prediction error," "free energy," and "posterior" from active inference; "parts," "Self," "protectors," "exiles," "blending," "unburdening" from IFS; and introduces "identity-level precision bundle," "capture index," "context-held activation," and "relational prediction error" as new terms. That is a lot of vocabulary, but the translation table and glossary manage it. The new terms are necessary and defined.

One concern: the paper is legible to computational psychiatry readers. It is less legible to pure IFS clinicians (who may not have active inference background) and to pure active inference theorists (who may not have IFS background). The paper wisely picks one primary audience (computational psychiatry) and writes for it. But the IFS clinician who encounters this paper will hit a wall at Section 3.1 unless they are unusually technically comfortable. That is acceptable for the target venue, but worth noting.

### D. Reusability
**Classification: concept + method + problem framing**

**Verdict: Strong | Severity: Minor issues only**

The paper produces at least three reusable assets:

1. **The concept of "identity-level precision bundle"** — a formal definition of "part" that is portable to any multiplicity-of-mind framework, not just IFS.
2. **The capture/context-held distinction** as a formal regime variable — portable to any clinical situation where the question is "is the patient identified with the content or relating to it."
3. **Relational prediction error** as a named mechanism — portable to any therapeutic context where the relational encounter (not just the informational correction) is the active ingredient.

All three are portable. Someone could cite the bundle definition without re-reading the whole paper. Someone could use the capture/context-held framing without endorsing the full IFS model. The relational prediction error concept could travel to attachment theory, psychedelic-assisted therapy, somatic work, or any context where "being met" is the hypothesized change agent.

The paper is also portable as a method template: "here is how you formalize a clinical framework using active inference." The formation, persistence, revision, and extension structure could be reused for other clinical phenomena.

Minor concern: the simulation code is referenced but not yet stable (v2 exists but the paper has not been updated to reflect it). The simulation as a reusable asset is not yet fully baked.

## Special Tests

### Clean move test

**Previous work assumed X:** Fear and threat models treat activation as the key variable; the question is whether the feared stimulus is contacted under conditions that permit extinction/updating. Self-state, if modeled at all, is a slowly-moving trait parameter, not an identity-level organizing prior.

**We show Y:** Parts are identity-level precision bundles with self-state upstream, Self-energy governs whether activation produces capture or context-held processing, and inside the context-held window, relational prediction error (not just informational correction) reaches the organizing prior.

**This matters because Z:** It explains why IFS produces upstream, generalizing change that exposure-based approaches do not predict, and it makes IFS's relational mechanism empirically testable rather than merely clinical intuition.

Assessment: The clean move works. X is stated clearly in the discriminant validity paragraph and in Section 8.5. Y is the paper's argument. Z is the empirical implication. The move is clean.

### Carry-forward sentence test

Candidate: "Parts are identity-level precision bundles in which self-state is the organizing prior — and revision that reaches the root generalizes in ways that revision of threat meaning alone cannot."

This sentence is portable. It is precise. It captures the central claim. It could be cited. It exists in the paper (Section 3, near the end). It could be punchier — the dash construction dilutes the force slightly — but it works.

Second candidate: "The part expected isolation. It encountered presence." (Section 8.3). This is more vivid and captures Move 3 specifically. It is not currently formatted as a standalone claim — it is embedded in a paragraph. If the paper's most citable insight is the relational prediction error mechanism, this sentence (or a version of it) should be promoted.

### Inevitability test

After reading the introduction, does the reader feel "yes, this paper now has to exist"?

**Mostly yes.** The opening three sentences create immediate pressure. The dog example grounds it. The gap ("no formal computational account of what a part actually is") is stated clearly. The roadmap is crisp.

What slightly weakens inevitability: the introduction promises three moves but does not yet make the reader feel the *cost* of not having this formalization. The gap is stated as an absence ("IFS has lacked a formal account") rather than as a problem with consequences. What goes wrong because the formalization does not exist? The paper could name one: "Without a formal account, the claim that IFS works differently from exposure remains unfalsifiable — clinically believed, scientifically invisible." That would make the paper feel not just useful but *necessary*.

### Beautiful ending test

**Does not yet pass.** The conclusion enumerates rather than gathers. The final sentence ("It builds the first floor. That is enough, at least for now, if the floor holds.") is honest and unpretentious, which is admirable. But the paper has done more than lay a floor. It has named a mechanism (relational prediction error reaching the organizing prior) that, if correct, changes how we understand therapeutic change across modalities. The ending should reflect the scale of that implication.

The conclusion currently reads like the ending of a methods paper. The paper deserves the ending of a theory paper — one that widens the aperture and lets the reader see what the framework makes visible for the first time. Not grandiosity. Recognition.

## What Prevents This From Feeling Iconic

1. **Move 3 is buried.** Relational prediction error (Section 8.3) is the paper's most original and consequential idea — it is what makes this an IFS paper rather than a generic active-inference-meets-clinical-psych paper. But it arrives in the second half, introduced as a subsection of a section about "why only context-held activation permits lasting change." The structural presentation says "this is one piece of the argument." The actual intellectual weight says "this is the argument." The paper does not yet know which of its three moves is the star.

2. **The conclusion does not arrive.** The paper earns a recognition that the conclusion does not deliver. After building a precise account of how relational prediction error reaches the organizing prior — something no prior formalization of any relational therapy has done — the paper ends with a humble floor-laying metaphor and a list of future work. The reader should leave feeling that something has been *shown*, not merely proposed.

3. **The simulation section is in limbo.** Sections 10-11 describe a v1 simulation while a v2 simulation exists. The paper currently asks the reader to trust results that are being replaced. This is a known gap, but it prevents the paper from having the full weight of a complete argument. Until the simulation sections match the actual simulation, the empirical backbone of the paper is provisional.

4. **The paper does not name the clinical surprise.** The formalism predicts things. The empirical targets in Section 12.4 are well-stated. But the paper never names a moment where the formalism tells the IFS clinician something they did not already know. That would be the moment that converts the paper from "a good translation exercise" to "a paper that changes practice." Candidates exist: the model predicts that the relational channel alone (witnessing without life-updating) should be sufficient for identity-level revision to begin. That is a clinical prediction with practical implications. But it is stated as a future empirical target rather than as a finding that the formalism itself delivers.

5. **The opening promise slightly exceeds the delivered scope.** The abstract promises that "active inference simulations confirm the ordering." The paper currently contains simulation results from v1 that support the claim, but the v2 simulation (which is described as showing clearer cascade, depth gap, and probe differentiation) has not been integrated. The paper claims confirmation from simulations that are in transit. The fix is simple (update Sections 10-11 to reflect v2), but until that is done, the paper slightly over-promises.
