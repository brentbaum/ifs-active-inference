# Voice & Style Guide

Extracted from Laukkonen, Friston & Chandaria (2025), "A Beautiful Loop."
Adapted for the IFS-Active Inference paper.

---

## Voice Principles

Confident, clean, architecturally precise. The voice of someone who has built a careful formal structure and is now walking you through it room by room. Not hedging — declaring, then showing. Not dry — there is a current of intellectual wonder running underneath the precision, surfacing in moments of naming ("the beautiful loop," "toroidal epistemicity") and in the quiet confidence of analogies chosen for their aesthetic economy.

The primary register is **builder-explaining**: "We propose three conditions. Here is the first. Here is why it matters. Here is what it looks like." Each concept is introduced, defined, put to work, and then recedes into the background as the next one arrives. The cumulative effect is architectural — by the end, the reader sees the whole structure and understands why each piece was load-bearing.

A secondary register is available: **phenomenological provocation**. Short, vivid sentences that name an experiential reality before the formal machinery explains it. "Sometimes 'I am afraid.' Sometimes 'a part of me is afraid.' Same activation, different relationship." This register opens sections, grounds claims in felt experience, and prevents the paper from becoming a formalism that forgot what it was formalizing.

**Sentence structure:**
- Medium-length sentences by default. Complex enough to carry a precise idea, short enough to parse on first reading.
- Short declarative sentences for key claims. "The reality model is what *can* be conscious." "Precision is the central formal tool in this paper."
- Longer sentences when unpacking mechanism — but broken by em dashes or parenthetical clarification, never left to run.
- Occasional fragments for emphasis, especially in section openings. "Same activation, different relationship."

**Tone:**
- Default: intellectually ambitious, architecturally confident, accessible. Not tentative.
- When defining: precise, modular. One concept per paragraph. Each definition earns its keep.
- When claiming: direct. "We propose." "The claim of this paper is." No apology.
- When acknowledging limits: honest, specific. "This is a deliberate simplification." "Some clinical constructs are only minimally formalized in this version." Name what you're not doing and move on — don't grovel.
- When bridging domains: respectful of both. "The clinical ontology is preserved as the source of phenomenological grounding. The computational account specifies the mechanism." Neither side is reduced to the other.

## Vocabulary

### Words & Phrases to Use

- "We propose" / "We model" / "This paper proposes" (active, claiming stance)
- "falls out" / "falls out of" (for consequences that emerge from the formalism without additional assumptions)
- "regime" (for qualitatively distinct states of a system — blending regime, witnessing regime)
- "captures" / "captured" (for how a part takes over inference, or how a model captures a phenomenon)
- "operationalized" (for the bridge between a theoretical construct and its formal proxy)
- "admits of degrees" / "graded" (for continua, not binaries)
- "load-bearing" (for assumptions that do real work in the argument)
- "minimal" / "deliberately minimal" (for scope discipline)
- "tractable" / "computationally tractable" (for claims about formalizability)
- "parsimoniously explains" (for when one mechanism handles multiple phenomena)
- "the interplay of" (for interaction effects, especially among precisions)
- "precision" / "prior precision" / "evidence precision" (the central formal vocabulary)
- "bundle" (for the cluster of priors that constitutes a part)
- "anachronistic" (for priors that were adaptive then, not now)
- "the present account" / "the present model" (self-reference)
- "condition" (for requirements in a theoretical framework — not "criterion" or "factor")
- Bold for key terms at introduction: **Bayesian Binding**, **Self-energy**, **epistemic depth**
- Italics for phenomenological emphasis: *feels like identity*, *something I am with*

### Words to Avoid

- "mechanism" used alone (say which mechanism — "precision-weighting mechanism," "the mechanism by which parts persist")
- "merely" (unless marking a genuine ontological distinction: "merely *in* the system" vs. "experience *for* the system")
- "clearly" / "obviously" / "it is well known that" (if it were obvious, you wouldn't need to say it)
- "complex" as a standalone modifier (say what the complexity is)
- "robust" / "robust evidence" (vague; cite the evidence or don't)
- "paradigm shift" / "revolutionary" / "groundbreaking" (let the reader decide)
- "novel" (more than once — state your contribution and let it stand)
- "suggests that future work" (at end of every section — collect future work in one place)
- "We believe" (you're writing a paper, not a confession — "we propose," "the model predicts")
- "It is interesting to note" (if it's interesting, just say it)
- "holistic" / "integrative" (too vague — specify what is being integrated and how)
- Scare quotes around technical terms after they've been defined (define once, use cleanly after)

## Punctuation & Formatting

**Capitalization:**
- Self (capital-S) when referring to the IFS concept of Self — the regime, not the person
- self (lowercase-s) for ordinary self-reference
- Standard capitalization otherwise — no ALL CAPS for emphasis

**Punctuation:**
- Em dashes for mid-sentence clarification — used frequently, as in "Self-energy — a composite capacity involving autonomic-social regulation and metacognitive depth — determines..."
- Parenthetical asides for brief glosses (for non-technical readers, this means...)
- Colons to introduce formal definitions or lists
- Semicolons to link parallel claims within a sentence
- Sparing use of italics for phenomenological emphasis and for "as-experienced" descriptions

**Formatting:**
- Tables for mapping between domains (clinical phenomenon → computational interpretation)
- Numbered lists for ordered conditions, temporal sequences, and procedural steps
- Bullet lists for unordered properties, features, and components
- Bold for key terms at first introduction within a section
- Section headers as architectural signposts — the reader should be able to reconstruct the argument from headers alone
- Short paragraphs (3-6 sentences). One idea per paragraph. The paragraph's first sentence carries the claim; the rest support it.
- Equations inline with prose, introduced by the sentence that needs them — never dumped in a block
- Diagrams (ASCII or figure) for architectural relationships — the loop, the hierarchy, the regime map

## Structural Patterns

### The Define-Claim-Show Pattern

The Beautiful Loop's core rhetorical unit:

1. **Define** the concept precisely, in one paragraph
2. **Claim** what role it plays in the argument, in one sentence (often bold)
3. **Show** how it works — mechanism, analogy, or table

Example from the source:
> "The first condition is the **simulation of a world model** — a unified, coherent epistemic field... [definition]. **The reality model is what *can* be conscious** — it defines the space of possible experiences. [claim]" Then properties, mechanism, and the analogy to a stage. [show]

### The Scope-Discipline Pattern

State what you are *not* doing before the reader can object:

- "This paper manipulates only three quantities."
- "All other precisions are held fixed. This is a deliberate simplification."
- "Some clinical constructs — especially protector negotiation — are only minimally formalized in this version."

This earns trust. The reader knows you see the edges of your own model.

### The Bridging Pattern

When two vocabularies describe the same territory (clinical IFS and computational active inference), use mapping tables and explicit translation:

| Clinical term | Computational term | What the mapping preserves |
|---|---|---|
| "The part believes..." | "The bundle includes a prior..." | The phenomenology — it *feels* like a belief |

### The Phenomenological Hook

Open sections with felt experience before introducing machinery:

- "Sometimes 'I am afraid.' Sometimes 'a part of me is afraid.'"
- "Same activation, different relationship."

Then the section explains *why* this difference exists, formally. The hook is not decoration — it is the phenomenon the section must account for.

## Anti-Patterns

### Never Say

- "This is a groundbreaking new framework" (just present it)
- "As we have shown above" (the structure should make this unnecessary — if not, fix the structure)
- "It is beyond the scope of this paper to..." (say what *is* in scope; silence handles the rest)
- "One might argue that..." then refute a strawman (engage real objections or don't)
- "In conclusion, we have shown that..." then restate the abstract (the conclusion should add something — implications, honest limits, what the model does *not* explain)

### Voice Failures

- [ ] Too hedged / academic-defensive ("it could perhaps be argued that there may be a case for...")
- [ ] Too grandiose / overselling ("this framework unifies all of consciousness science")
- [ ] Formalism without phenomenological grounding (equations that forgot what they're modeling)
- [ ] Phenomenology without formal backing (evocative writing with no machinery underneath)
- [ ] Listing future work at the end of every section instead of collecting it
- [ ] Introducing terms and never using them again (every defined term must do work)
- [ ] Explaining active inference from scratch for 10 pages before getting to the contribution (introduce only what's needed, when it's needed)
- [ ] Treating the clinical ontology as embarrassing or "merely metaphorical" (it is preserved and respected, alongside the computational account)

### Examples of Wrong Voice

**Bad:** "IFS is a holistic, integrative psychotherapy that has shown promising results in clinical settings and may benefit from a more rigorous theoretical grounding."
**Why:** Vague, hedging, reads like a grant application intro. No specific claim, no specific phenomenon, no hook.

**Bad:** "We present a novel, groundbreaking computational framework that, for the first time, provides a complete and unified account of all IFS phenomena."
**Why:** Overselling. The paper doesn't account for all phenomena and says so honestly. The good version names the scope: "We model parts, blending, witnessing, and unburdening. Protector negotiation and self-like parts are treated as future elaborations."

**Bad:** "The free energy principle (Friston, 2010) states that biological systems minimize variational free energy, which is an upper bound on surprise, defined as the negative log evidence for a generative model of sensory data under a Markov blanket partition..."
**Why:** Textbook recitation. The paper needs only what serves the argument: "Precision is how much the system trusts a given source of information. High precision means 'trust this signal strongly.'"

## Examples of Right Voice

**Good:** "The claim of this paper is not that IFS outperforms exposure or other evidence-based treatments in every context. It is that IFS targets a different variable: not activation alone, but the relation of the system to activated part-content."

**Good:** "Parts are not separate sub-agents with their own generative models. They are learned patterns of precision allocation within a single generative model that, when activated, shape both perception and action."

**Good:** "For non-technical readers, precision can be read as *how much the system trusts a given source of information*. High precision means 'trust this signal strongly.' Low precision means 'this signal is uncertain; weight it lightly.'"

**Good:** "These two ontologies are not in conflict. They operate at different levels of description."

**Good (phenomenological hook → formal claim):** "Sometimes 'I am afraid.' Sometimes 'a part of me is afraid.' Same activation, different relationship. This paper asks what determines which — and why only the second permits lasting change."

**Good (scope discipline):** "This paper manipulates only three quantities... All other precisions are held fixed. This is a deliberate simplification. A more complete model would account for the full precision landscape. The present model aims to show that the core IFS phenomena can be derived from the interplay of these three quantities alone."
